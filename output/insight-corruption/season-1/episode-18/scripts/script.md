## Segment 1 — Cold Open (0:00–2:15)

There is a sentence buried in an Oakland Public Ethics Commission record that changes the way you read a housing file.

The record concerns a City building inspector named Thomas Espinosa. The hearing officer found that Espinosa committed forty-three violations of Oakland ethics laws during 2015 and 2016. Among the conduct described in that case were four separate three-hundred-dollar payments involving a landlord named Elizabeth Williams — payments the hearing officer found Espinosa solicited in exchange for permits, inspections, green tags, and other official acts.

That is not an allegation from social media. It is not a rumor. It is not somebody's memory of what happened. It is part of an adjudicated municipal ethics case.

But here is where this story gets complicated.

Years later, Williams's name appears again in a very different set of records — this time connected to a publicly funded veteran housing placement in Oakland administered through Insight Housing's Supportive Services for Veteran Families program.

And in 2026, after the placement broke down, an Oakland police report recorded allegations involving housing conditions, public funds, an eviction, and the way complaints were handled.

Those later allegations have not been adjudicated as criminal findings. That distinction matters. We are going to keep it visible throughout this episode.

Because this is not a story where the responsible thing is to mash two sets of records together and announce a conspiracy.

It is a story about what happens when a documented public-integrity warning from one era intersects with a publicly funded housing program years later — and about the basic oversight question that follows:

Who checked the paper trail before the money moved?

This is *Insight Corruption*.

## Segment 2 — The 2015–2016 Oakland Ethics Record (2:15–14:28)

Start with the strongest source in the file.

Oakland Public Ethics Commission Case Number 16-14 concerned Thomas Espinosa, who worked as a Specialty Combination Inspector in Oakland's Department of Planning and Building. In plain English, he was a City building inspector.

The hearing officer's findings say Espinosa worked for the City from 2005 to 2016. Applying a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard, the hearing officer concluded that during 2015 and 2016 Espinosa committed forty-three violations of Oakland ethics laws involving bribery, misuse of City authority, conflicts of interest, misuse of public resources, and failures to make required disclosures.

The Williams portion of the record is unusually specific.

The hearing officer described Elizabeth Williams as a landlord who owned several rental properties in Oakland. The findings also state that the City had sued her multiple times over failures to adequately maintain properties.

Then the decision lays out four transactions.

In November 2015, Espinosa solicited three hundred dollars in connection with permits for a Market Street property.

In January 2016, the record says he solicited another three hundred dollars in connection with an inspection and permit pass for a Mead Street property.

In March 2016, the findings describe two more three-hundred-dollar solicitations tied to permits and rough and final inspections for a property on 27th Street.

The hearing officer concluded that those solicitations violated Oakland's Government Ethics Act because a City employee may not solicit or accept something of value in exchange for performing an official act.

The same decision also describes another fifteen-hundred-dollar payment involving a real-estate broker and a separate property, but that is a different branch of the case.

For this episode, the important fact is narrower.

Elizabeth Williams was not a random name added to the record years later. She appears directly in a formal Oakland ethics proceeding involving the permit-and-inspection system.

Now, precision matters here.

The ethics findings are against Espinosa. The document does not make this episode a criminal conviction of Williams. It establishes that the City's ethics process found Espinosa solicited and received money from her in exchange for official acts.

That distinction is not a technicality. It is the difference between reporting what the government actually found and inventing a stronger conclusion than the record supports.

There's more to the record than the hearing officer's recommendation alone. Press coverage of the Commission's final vote, in September 2021, describes the fine being raised to three hundred nine thousand six hundred dollars — the largest ethics fine the Commission had ever issued — across a reported forty-seven violations. That's a higher count than the forty-three in the hearing-officer document we're relying on here, and we have not yet obtained the Commission's own final written decision to reconcile the two numbers, so we're naming both, with their sources.

That same reporting describes something beyond the four three-hundred-dollar solicitations: Espinosa was also paid to work as Williams's contractor on her rental properties, then inspected his own work and closed at least one code case against her without disclosing the conflict. Reports disagree on the total — one outlet says one hundred seventy-six thousand dollars, another says roughly one hundred twelve thousand — and neither figure is verified here against the Commission's financial exhibits.

And Espinosa wasn't the only inspector named. A second building inspector, Anthony Harbaugh, was separately found to have taken a three-hundred-dollar payment from Espinosa to approve electrical work at a Williams property after two other inspectors had turned it down, and to have failed to report income he received from Williams. Harbaugh was fined fifty-five thousand dollars. One outlet also reports Williams received immunity from the FBI to testify about the scheme — we haven't independently confirmed that against a federal record, so treat it as reported, not established.

There's independent corroboration of this landlord's history outside the ethics file, too. In a separate small-claims arbitration that aired as an episode of the show Judge Faith, a tenant named Danielle Johnson sued Williams over habitability conditions at a different property — a ceiling that gave way during renovation, a roof leak that soaked her bed during a storm, and a sewage backup the City had documented — and won a judgment of fifteen hundred twenty-five dollars after the arbitrator found Williams had breached the legal warranty of habitability. In that same hearing, Johnson testified that the City of Oakland had sued Williams over eleven properties back in 2006. That's her sworn testimony, not something we've independently matched to a court filing ourselves, but it lines up with a separate 2018 news account describing that same lawsuit.

So why does the old ethics file matter to a housing story years later?

Because publicly funded housing systems depend on screening.

They depend on someone verifying that a unit is appropriate, that a landlord is eligible, that inspections are real, that public money is tied to actual services, and that known risk indicators are not simply ignored because the program needs a unit quickly.

The ethics case does not answer whether any later housing program knew about Williams's history in the file.

It raises the question.

And that question becomes much more significant once the later SSVF records enter the timeline.

## Segment 3 — The Publicly Funded Placement (14:28–19:48)

Fast-forward to December 2024.

Records retained by the reporting party show approval for financial support through the Supportive Services for Veteran Families program, commonly called SSVF. The placement was administered through Insight Housing.

The approved assistance described in those records included housing-related financial support. The reporting party was placed at 1055 72nd Avenue in Oakland, at a property associated in the later file with Elizabeth Williams.

This is the point where a public-integrity question becomes a program-compliance question.

When a nonprofit or contractor helps place a veteran using federal housing funds, the public has a legitimate interest in understanding what due diligence occurred before the placement.

Was the property inspected?

Who performed the inspection?

What checklist was used?

Were code-enforcement records reviewed?

Were outstanding violations identified?

Were repairs required before move-in?

Did anyone search prior City enforcement records connected to the owner or the property?

And if not, why not?

The records currently assembled for this episode do not answer all of those questions.

That is important to say clearly.

There are references in the complainant's later police report to ACCELA records, City violation notices dating to 2019, inspector notes, and fire-inspection records. But a police report referencing those materials is not the same thing as independently validating every underlying record.

So those documents belong in the next layer of reporting, not in the narration as settled facts until they are matched directly.

What we can say is that the placement happened, public housing assistance was involved, and the later dispute became serious enough to generate police reports, court filings, records requests, and demands for independent review.

The reporting party's retained correspondence also describes complaints about security and habitability conditions. One 2025 email from Insight Housing staff memorialized concerns that included pest issues, lack of security, an unsecured gate or fence, people sleeping in bushes, access beneath the building, and stolen packages.

That email matters because it is not merely the tenant saying later, "I complained."

It is an internal service-provider record reflecting that the complaints had been communicated.

But even here, the right question is not simply whether a complaint existed.

The question is what happened after the complaint.

Was the unit reinspected?

Were defects corrected?

Was the landlord required to respond?

Did the housing provider document resolution?

And if the answer is no, what happened to the public money while the underlying service problem remained unresolved?

That is the accountability lane of this story.

Not assumption. Documentation.

## Segment 5 — Complaint, Eviction, and the 2026 Police Report (21:18–28:08)

By early 2026, the housing relationship had deteriorated.

The reporting party says he escalated complaints about the placement and SSVF services through multiple channels, including the VA and Insight Housing.

A retained chronology places a complaint to the VA National Homeless Veterans Hotline on January 4, 2026. Four days later, on January 8, an unlawful-detainer action was initiated concerning the property.

That timing is part of the complainant's retaliation theory.

It is not, by itself, proof of retaliation.

Timing can be evidence. It can raise questions. But the legal meaning of that timing depends on facts that would need to be established through the court record, communications, and the reasons given for the eviction.

Later in January, the eviction took effect, according to records cited in the complainant's timeline.

In February, the reporting party continued escalating concerns about program administration and the handling of housing assistance.

Then, on May 17, 2026, an Oakland Police Department report was filed.

The report lists the incident type as theft and identifies several people as subjects of the report, including Insight Housing personnel, Elizabeth Williams, and others connected to the veteran-services system.

The narrative supplied by the reporting party alleges fraud involving federal housing funds, the condition of the property, the eviction, and later adverse actions.

Again: this report proves that these allegations were submitted to the police. It does not prove the allegations themselves.

That distinction is especially important because the report uses criminal-law labels — including theories involving fraud, theft, conspiracy, and retaliation.

Those labels are the reporting party's allegations for investigative review. They are not findings of guilt.

What makes the report relevant to this episode is not that it settles the dispute.

It is that it identifies the records that should be examined if anyone wants to settle the factual questions.

The report references an ACCELA record, older violation notices, inspector notes, fire-inspection records, SSVF approval and disbursement records, the unlawful-detainer filings, the Oakland ethics case, and transfer or payment records.

That is a roadmap.

And roadmaps can be tested.

For example:

If the SSVF ledger shows exactly how much public money was paid, to whom, on what dates, and for what authorized purpose, then one major part of the dispute becomes verifiable.

If inspection records show when the property was evaluated and what conditions were documented, another piece becomes verifiable.

If ACCELA and fire records show unresolved violations before the placement, then the question becomes whether the housing provider knew or reasonably should have known.

If those records instead show the property passed all relevant requirements and defects arose later, that changes the story too.

Good investigative reporting has to leave room for either result.

Because the goal is not to make the documents fit a theory.

The goal is to make the theory answer to the documents.

## Segment 6 — What Insight Housing Controls, and What It Does Not (28:08–32:13)

There is another reason to keep the roles straight.

Insight Housing is not the Oakland Building Department. It does not issue City permits. It did not adjudicate the Espinosa ethics case.

Its role in the later chapter is different: housing placement, services, program administration, and the handling of publicly funded assistance.

That creates a separate set of accountability questions.

What standards did Insight Housing use when approving the unit?

What did its staff know about the property?

What records did it review?

What happened after documented complaints arrived?

What did its internal case notes say?

Were inspection commitments fulfilled?

Were financial-support commitments fulfilled?

Was there a process for a participant to challenge a decision without losing access to services?

And when the relationship collapsed, what information was shared among the nonprofit, the VA, the landlord, or other actors?

These are questions for documents and right-of-reply responses.

They should not be answered by implication.

The same is true for Williams.

The old PEC file is relevant because it documents her involvement in transactions that became part of a corruption finding against a City inspector.

But the later SSVF story requires its own proof.

Did Williams receive public money connected to the 72nd Avenue placement?

If so, how much?

Under what contract or rental arrangement?

What representations were made about the unit?

What inspections occurred?

What repairs were requested?

What notices were served?

What is her response to the allegation that the property had unresolved issues before or during the placement?

Those questions belong in a right-of-reply request before publication.

And this is the part of corruption reporting people often skip.

The old red flag is not the new conviction.

It is the reason you know where to look.

## Segment 7 — The Oversight Failure Question (32:13–35:38)

So what is the actual public-interest issue here?

It is larger than one landlord and one nonprofit.

Governments increasingly rely on networks of contractors, landlords, nonprofits, case managers, and service providers to deliver housing assistance.

That can move people into housing faster than a centralized bureaucracy.

But it also fragments responsibility.

The landlord says the provider chose the placement.

The provider says the unit met program requirements.

The funding agency says the contractor administers the service.

The City says federal housing funds are outside one department's lane.

The veteran or tenant sits in the middle trying to figure out who is actually responsible when something goes wrong.

That is where accountability disappears.

A functioning oversight system should be able to answer basic questions without a resident filing police reports and public-records requests for months.

Who approved the unit?

Who inspected it?

Who paid the money?

Who received it?

What conditions were known?

What complaints were made?

What corrective action occurred?

And who had authority to stop payments or move the participant if the placement was not working?

Those are not exotic questions.

They are the minimum controls you would expect around public money and vulnerable residents.

The older Oakland ethics case adds one more question:

When a landlord or property already appears in a meaningful City enforcement or integrity record, does the housing-placement system check?

If the answer is yes, show the record.

If the answer is no, that is a policy gap worth fixing.
