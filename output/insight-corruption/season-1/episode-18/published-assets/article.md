# Elizabeth Williams & Insight Housing: The Paper Trail

## An Oakland ethics case, a publicly funded housing placement, and the oversight questions connecting them

A 2021 Oakland Public Ethics Commission decision contains a detail that deserves renewed scrutiny in light of a later publicly funded housing dispute.

The ethics case concerned Thomas Espinosa, a City of Oakland building inspector. A hearing officer found that Espinosa committed 43 violations of Oakland ethics laws during 2015 and 2016, including bribery, misuse of City authority, conflicts of interest, misuse of public resources, and failures to make mandatory financial disclosures. Press coverage of the Commission's own final vote that September describes a broader outcome still — a fine raised to $309,600, the largest the Commission had ever issued, across a reported 47 violations. This article has not obtained the Commission's own final written decision to reconcile that higher count against the hearing officer's 43, so both figures are presented here with their source.

One section of the hearing officer's record concerns Oakland landlord Elizabeth Williams.

According to the findings, Espinosa solicited and received four separate $300 payments from Williams in connection with permits and inspections at Oakland properties — at addresses on Market Street, Mead Street, and 27th Street. The decision concluded that those solicitations violated Oakland's Government Ethics Act because a City employee may not solicit or accept something of value in exchange for an official act.

Reporting on the Commission's final decision describes a second, separate financial relationship: Espinosa was also paid to work as Williams's electrical or general contractor on her rental properties, then inspected his own work and closed at least one code-enforcement case against her, in October 2015, without disclosing the conflict on his required financial-disclosure form. The reported total of those contractor payments is inconsistent across outlets — Oaklandside reports $176,000; East Bay Express reports approximately $112,000 — and neither figure is independently verified here against the Commission's underlying financial exhibits.

A second Oakland building inspector, Anthony Harbaugh, was separately found by a PEC hearing officer to have accepted a $300 payment from Espinosa to approve electrical work at a Williams property after two other inspectors had rejected it, and to have failed to report income he received from Williams between January 2015 and September 2016. Harbaugh was fined $55,000. East Bay Express additionally reports that Williams was granted immunity by the FBI in exchange for testimony about the scheme — that has not been independently confirmed here against a Department of Justice or FBI record, and is presented as reported, not established.

The findings also describe Williams as a landlord who owned several Oakland rental properties and whose properties had previously been the subject of City maintenance litigation. That history has two further, independent points of corroboration. First, a single 2018 account (Indybay) describes tenants alleging an electrical fire at a different Williams-associated property displaced three units, and a separate tenant alleging Harbaugh was personally close to Williams and refused a requested verification inspection — an allegation not corroborated against fire-department incident records or a second outlet, and treated here as unverified. Second, and more solidly: in a small-claims arbitration aired as an episode of *Judge Faith* ("Johnson v. Williams"), tenant Danielle Johnson — the same tenant Indybay named in 2018, at a different property — won a $1,525 judgment against Williams after the arbitrator found she had breached the implied warranty of habitability, citing a renovation-related ceiling collapse, a roof leak that soaked Johnson's bed, a second bathroom-ceiling collapse, and a City-documented sewage backup. In sworn testimony during that hearing, Johnson also said the City of Oakland had sued Williams over 11 properties in 2006 — consistent with, though not independently matched here to, the 2006 lawsuit Indybay separately reported. *Insight Corruption* has since identified and reached out to Johnson to ask whether she is willing to give witness testimony for this episode; that outreach is pending.

That is the documented historical record, across an adjudicated finding, press coverage of the Commission's fuller decision, and independent tenant accounts.

The important limitation is equally clear: these are findings against Espinosa and Harbaugh, and reporting about their conduct — not criminal findings against Williams, and not proof of the later allegations below.

## The later SSVF placement

Years later, Williams's name appears again in records connected to a publicly funded veteran housing placement administered through Insight Housing's Supportive Services for Veteran Families program, or SSVF.

In December 2024, records retained by the housing-program participant show that he was approved for financial support through SSVF and placed through Insight Housing at 1055 72nd Avenue in Oakland. Later records associate the property with Elizabeth Williams. The participant's records describe public housing assistance connected to the placement and commitments that included financial support and housing-related services.

That later chapter is not an adjudicated corruption case. It is a dispute supported by a mix of program records, correspondence, court-related materials, public-records efforts, and an Oakland Police Department report containing allegations submitted by the participant. The older ethics case does not prove the later allegations. But it raises a legitimate public-interest question: what due diligence and screening occurred before public money supported a housing placement associated with a landlord who already appeared in a significant municipal ethics record?

A follow-up records link now answers part of that question directly: **Oakland ACCELA Case No. 2600830**, for the property at 1055 72nd Avenue (parcel 041 414600600), is confirmed open, with a status of "OP-Insp-Not Abated." Its narrative references a January 4, 2026 complaint to the VA's National Homeless Veterans Hotline and a January 8, 2026 summons, consistent with the participant's own chronology. The case's full prior inspection and violation history has not yet been pulled, and a related public-records request (NextRequest 26-7112) could not be reviewed past its landing page in this pass.

The remaining public-interest questions are about screening. What inspection occurred before move-in? Who performed it? What code-enforcement or fire records were reviewed? Were outstanding violations identified? Was the landlord's prior history in City records considered relevant to placement approval? The materials reviewed so far do not answer every one of those questions, and that absence is itself significant, because these are basic controls expected in a publicly funded housing program.

## Documented complaints during the placement

The participant later reported recurring security and habitability concerns. Retained correspondence from Insight Housing reflects complaints involving pest issues, lack of security, an unsecured gate or fence, people sleeping in bushes near the property, access beneath the building, and package theft. That correspondence is important because it shows that at least some concerns were documented within the service-provider relationship rather than raised only after the housing dispute escalated.

The unresolved question is what corrective action followed. Was the unit reinspected? Were repairs ordered? Was the landlord required to respond? Were complaints resolved in writing? Were public payments affected by the condition of the unit? Those answers should exist in inspection files, case notes, payment records, or internal communications if the program was operating with a documented compliance process.

## The 2026 police report

On May 17, 2026, the participant filed an Oakland Police Department report concerning the housing dispute. The report lists several people and organizations connected to the matter, including Insight Housing personnel and Elizabeth Williams. The narrative submitted by the reporting party alleges misconduct involving public housing funds, the condition of the property, the eviction process, and later adverse actions.

Those allegations have not been treated here as proven criminal conduct. A police report establishes that allegations were made. It does not establish that the allegations are true. That distinction is especially important because the narrative references criminal theories involving fraud, theft, conspiracy, and retaliation — allegations for investigative review, not findings of guilt.

The more useful part of the report is its record map. It references Oakland ACCELA materials, older violation notices, inspector notes, fire-inspection records, SSVF approval and disbursement records, unlawful-detainer filings, the earlier Public Ethics Commission matter, and payment records. Those materials can be tested against the allegations. If they contradict the participant's account, the public record should reflect that. If they corroborate it, that matters too.

## Why Insight Housing's role requires separate scrutiny

Insight Housing was not part of the Espinosa ethics case. Its role arises years later, through housing placement, case management, service delivery, and administration of public assistance. That creates a different set of accountability questions: What landlord-screening process did the organization use? What property inspection standards applied? What did staff know about prior City records? How were participant complaints handled? Were promised inspections or conflict-resolution services actually performed? What public funds were authorized and paid, and to whom? What independent appeal or complaint process existed for a participant who challenged program decisions?

These questions should be answerable without speculation. They are questions of contracting, compliance, documentation, and public-fund stewardship.

## The oversight gap

Publicly funded housing programs often operate through multiple layers: government agencies, nonprofits, landlords, case managers, inspectors, and outside service providers. That structure can help move people into housing quickly. It can also fragment responsibility when something goes wrong. A landlord may point to the nonprofit. The nonprofit may point to program standards. The funding agency may point to the grantee. The City may say a federal program is outside one department's jurisdiction. The resident is left trying to reconstruct the system through emails, police reports, court filings, and public-records requests.

A functioning oversight system should already know:

- who approved the unit;
- who inspected it;
- what conditions were documented;
- who authorized the money;
- who received the money;
- what complaints were filed;
- what corrective action followed; and
- who had authority to suspend payments or move the participant if the placement failed.

Those are not extraordinary demands. They are basic controls around public money and vulnerable residents.

## The central question

The older Oakland ethics file does not establish what happened in the later SSVF placement. It establishes why deeper diligence is reasonable. When a landlord or property appears in a meaningful municipal enforcement or integrity record, does a publicly funded housing-placement system check? If it does, the documentation should show what was reviewed and what conclusion was reached. If it does not, the gap deserves attention.

That is the question at the center of this episode of *Insight Corruption*. Not whether one old ethics case automatically proves a new crime. Whether public institutions learned enough from the old record to protect public funds and the people those funds were supposed to serve.
