It is worth pausing on why a role like Finance Director carries this kind of trust in the first place. When they don't, the failure is quiet by nature: nobody votes to approve an embezzlement, so nobody notices a vote was never needed for the money to move.

## The Restitution Strategy

Clarke Howatt was sentenced to one year and one day in federal prison, followed by six months of home confinement and three years of supervised release. He and his family paid three and a half million dollars in restitution — most, though notably not all, of the nearly four million dollars taken.

One year and one day is a specific, deliberate-sounding number in federal sentencing, and it is worth explaining why. Under federal law, a sentence of more than one year generally requires a defendant to serve it with reduced credit for good behavior calculated differently than a sentence of one year or less; "a year and a day" is a widely recognized marker in federal sentencing outcomes, not a coincidence of the calendar. Whatever the specific guideline calculation behind it in this case, the resulting sentence put Howatt's actual served time near the low end of what a felony of this scale could have produced.

The restitution order — family money used to repay a large share of what was stolen — is the other half of the outcome. Paying back the money does not undo the delay to the housing projects those funds were meant to support, and it does not, on its own, explain why a four-year, multi-million-dollar theft from a public agency resulted in close to one year served rather than a sentence measured in years. What the restitution order does establish is that a meaningful portion of the stolen money was recovered by the public agency — which is a real, if partial, form of accountability, separate from and not a substitute for the criminal sentence itself.

It is worth noting what the restitution figure does not cover. Three and a half million dollars against nearly three point nine million taken leaves a gap of roughly four hundred thousand dollars unrecovered, on the public record's own numbers. That gap rarely gets mentioned in coverage that leads with the larger, rounder figures on both sides. It matters because full restitution and near-full restitution are not the same claim, and a podcast built around claim-level precision should say so plainly: the public agency did not get all of its money back, even after the sentence most listeners would describe as "he paid it back."

## Why Restitution Moves a Federal Sentence

It is worth stepping back from this specific case to explain a pattern this show sees repeatedly in public-corruption and embezzlement sentencing: restitution genuinely matters to how a federal sentence gets calculated, and it is important to understand why, rather than just react to the outcome.

Federal law provides for mandatory restitution in many fraud and theft cases, separate from and in addition to incarceration. That is not a loophole — Congress wrote restitution into federal law specifically so that victims, including public agencies, have a legal mechanism to recover losses regardless of what prison term is imposed.

Where restitution becomes relevant to the length of incarceration is through the broader sentencing factors judges apply under 18 U.S.C. Section 3553(a): the nature and circumstances of the offense, the defendant's history and characteristics, the need for the sentence to reflect the seriousness of the offense and provide just punishment, the need for deterrence, and the need to protect the public. A defendant who repays a substantial share of what was taken — particularly quickly, and particularly using personal or family resources rather than resisting collection — is often treated by courts as demonstrating acceptance of responsibility and reducing the ongoing harm to the victim, both of which can weigh toward a lower sentence within the applicable guideline range.

The important distinction this show wants to draw is this: restitution being legally relevant to a sentence is not the same question as whether restitution should be enough, on its own, to substantially reduce time served for a multi-million-dollar theft from a public agency serving vulnerable populations. That second question is a policy judgment, not a legal requirement — and it is exactly the kind of question a case like Clarke Howatt's puts in front of listeners without a single right answer already built in.

It helps to be precise about what restitution is not. It is not a fine that converts prison time into a price. It is not, legally, a bargain a defendant strikes to buy down a sentence. It is a court-ordered repayment to a victim, calculated separately from — and, in principle, independent of — the punishment component of a federal sentence. But in practice, the two are not experienced as fully independent by anyone watching a sentencing outcome from the outside, including this show's listeners, because a defendant's demonstrated willingness and ability to make the victim close to whole is one of the very factors judges are instructed to weigh under Section 3553(a). That is the mechanism by which restitution, without ever being officially a sentence-reduction tool, ends up functioning like one in cases exactly like this.

## What Would Have Caught This Sooner

Set the Howatt case down next to how public-sector financial controls are generally supposed to work, and the four-year timeline becomes the most interesting fact in the whole file.

Standard public-agency financial control depends on a handful of unglamorous practices: separation of duties, so the same person who initiates a payment cannot also approve and reconcile it; independent, rotating external audits that sample transactions rather than trusting the same internal team's sign-off year after year; positive-pay banking controls that flag disbursements outside expected patterns; and a whistleblower channel that protects a junior employee who notices something wrong from having to risk their job to report it.

None of those controls are exotic. They are standard practice at well-run public agencies, required in many cases by state and federal grant conditions attached to the very kind of housing funding ABAG was administering. A four-year, multi-million-dollar diversion by one person, in one role, is the kind of outcome that ordinarily indicates one or more of those standard controls either did not exist, was not followed, or was not tested by an independent party during that period.

This show is not in a position to say which control failed at ABAG specifically, because that level of internal detail is not part of the public reporting compiled for this episode. But naming the general category of failure — inadequate separation of duties or inadequate independent testing, somewhere in that four-year window — is a more useful accountability question for listeners than simply repeating the dollar figure. A future season update to this episode should specifically look for whether ABAG published a post-incident control review, and if so, what it found.

It's worth being specific about why segregation of duties matters so much in exactly this kind of case. The core idea is simple: no single person should be able to both authorize a payment and be the one who verifies that the payment was proper. When one role holds both functions — as a Finance Director's role often does by default, unless deliberately structured otherwise — the checks that are supposed to catch an error or a theft never actually leave that one person's desk. An independent auditor sampling transactions after the fact is a partial substitute, but sampling means most transactions in most years are never individually reviewed by anyone outside the finance function itself. That is not a hypothetical weakness. It is close to a structural description of how a four-year, multi-million-dollar diversion by a single Finance Director could occur without being caught by routine process.

## A Pattern This Season Keeps Finding

This is the second case this season, and it will not be the last, where restitution and a defendant's personal resources shape a sentence more visibly than the scale of the original theft does.

The pattern is not that restitution is illegitimate — federal law requires it for good reason, and a victim, including a public agency, recovering stolen money is a real form of accountability that a prison sentence alone does not provide. The pattern this series keeps documenting is narrower: when a defendant or their family has the resources to make a large, fast restitution payment, that capacity appears to correlate with a meaningfully shorter sentence than cases where a defendant lacks those resources, even when the underlying offense conduct is comparably serious.

That correlation, if it holds up across more cases this season, raises a fairness question that isn't really about Clarke Howatt individually. It's about whether the federal system, in practice, ends up sentencing wealth and family financial capacity almost as much as it sentences conduct — a defendant with money behind them can make restitution fast and demonstrate the acceptance-of-responsibility narrative that favorably shapes a sentence, while a defendant without that access cannot, regardless of how each one actually behaved.

This show will keep tracking that pattern case by case, rather than asserting it as a conclusion from one data point. But it is exactly the kind of question a single case like this one is built to raise, even if it can't be built to answer on its own.
