# 11 — Why Dollar Amounts Alone Don't Compare Sentences

**Segment ID:** comparative-caution
**Type:** analysis
**Words:** 354
**Estimated runtime:** 2:26
**Claim IDs:** C06

It is tempting to compare this case to others purely by the numbers: nearly four million dollars stolen, about one year served. Compared to other financial-crime sentences a listener may have heard about — some far longer for smaller amounts, some shorter for larger ones — the temptation is to conclude the system is simply inconsistent or unfair based on that one comparison.

A responsible comparison needs more than a dollar figure and a sentence length. It needs to account for the specific charges Howatt pleaded to or was convicted of, his criminal history, whether the offense involved obstruction or additional charges, how quickly and fully restitution was made, whether cooperation with investigators played a role, and how the specific federal sentencing guidelines calculated the base offense level and any adjustments for this exact case. None of those variables are fully detailed in the public reporting compiled for this episode.

That is not a reason to avoid asking whether the outcome was fair. It is a reason to ask the comparison question the right way: not "was this sentence shorter than some other case's," but "what would we need to know about both cases to say they should have been treated the same, and were they?" That is a harder, more useful question — and it is one this series will keep returning to as more Bay Area corruption cases are examined side by side.

It is also worth resisting the opposite temptation: assuming that because a full apples-to-apples comparison isn't available in the public record, no judgment can be made at all. That isn't right either. Even without every guideline-calculation detail, the shape of this case is knowable and the shape is what this episode has tried to describe honestly — a large, sustained theft from a public housing fund, met with a sentence near the low end of what such an offense could produce, substantially shaped by a restitution payment that recovered most, but not all, of what was taken. Listeners can reasonably ask whether that shape reflects the right balance between accountability and proportional punishment, even without a spreadsheet of comparable cases to point to.
