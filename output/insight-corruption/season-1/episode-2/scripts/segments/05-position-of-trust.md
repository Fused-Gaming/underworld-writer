# 05 — A Trusted Seat Inside Regional Government

**Segment ID:** position-of-trust
**Type:** case-specific
**Words:** 441
**Estimated runtime:** 3:02
**Claim IDs:** C01

The Association of Bay Area Governments — ABAG — is not a city hall most people can picture. It is a regional planning body that coordinates housing and land-use policy across the nine-county Bay Area. It does not run on a small budget; regional housing programs move real money, and someone has to be trusted to manage it.

As of 2016, that person was Clarke Howatt, ABAG's Finance Director. A finance director's job, by definition, is access: access to accounts, to disbursement approvals, to the internal financial controls meant to catch exactly this kind of theft. That access is also what made the embezzlement possible for as long as it reportedly went on.

This episode is not going to speculate about internal motive beyond what the public record establishes. What the record does establish is narrower and more useful: a senior financial administrator, inside a regional government body responsible for housing funds, was later found to have diverted millions of dollars from that agency over a period of years. The rest of this episode follows what happened once that was discovered.

It is worth pausing on why a role like Finance Director carries this kind of trust in the first place. Regional government bodies like ABAG are governed by boards made up of elected officials from member cities and counties, most of whom have full-time jobs running their own jurisdictions. Day-to-day financial administration is, by necessity, delegated to staff. That delegation is not a design flaw — no regional government could function if every disbursement required board-level sign-off — but it does mean the system depends heavily on the internal controls surrounding that delegated authority actually working as intended. When they don't, the failure is quiet by nature: nobody votes to approve an embezzlement, so nobody notices a vote was never needed for the money to move.

It's also worth naming what a position like this is not. Clarke Howatt was not an elected official. He did not run for office, was not subject to a campaign-finance disclosure regime, and was not a household name to the residents whose housing projects his agency's money was meant to support. That is precisely why this case belongs in a series about public trust rather than a narrower series about elected corruption: the people who quietly administer public money day to day are, in aggregate, just as consequential to whether that money reaches its intended purpose as the officials who vote to approve the budgets in the first place. A resident who diligently follows city council votes and campaign contributions can still be blindsided by a theft that happened entirely inside the unelected administrative layer beneath those votes.
